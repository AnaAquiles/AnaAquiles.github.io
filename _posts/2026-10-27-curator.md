>*... nous verrons l'imagination construire des « murs » avec
des ombres impalpables, se réconforter avec des illusions de protection — ou, inversement trembler derrière des murs épais, douter des
plus solides remparts. - **Gaston Bachelar, La poétique de l'espace**.*

I've been thinking for a while about the similarities between scientists and artists. Of course, I don't mean to diminish either one as a creative and professional pursuit, but I do want to lay out what, from my point of view, unites us as "exhibitors."

My background in the artistic world comes from an amateur's seat in the audience, where I feed my curiosity about the creative process through books, discussions, podcasts, and exhibitions too. But what has left me most certain of the following points of convergence has been all the talks and discussions about an artist's life and experience: how they live, how they suffer, how they approach those who look at them with curiosity, or perhaps how they pull away, afraid of being seen through their first creations, and at the end of the day, how they try not to lose their sanity while they keep extending a part of themselves to the audience.

Listening recently to an episode of [Arte en Diálogo](https://youtu.be/hUAiq46JvdA?si=pCwiYueTrP8rry-i) about *magic in art*, I also heard about the vicissitudes a visual artist goes through in trying to stay in a creative, investigative (*why not*) and profound state, which inevitably brings solitude and isolation. The artist mentioned that nobody warns you how intense creating is: "the practice of art and studio work is hard to sustain on an existential level. It is hard on a psychic level. Hard because it stays at the edge of what Western society allows, [...] it keeps you exposed to other people's opinions."
By contrast, although I don't find much behind these words, the scientist also faces an existential isolation, in which for weeks they do nothing but read scientific literature on one specific topic, write, get frustrated and, if given the chance, exchange ideas with a colleague, though often dampened by frustration, criticism and intellectual competition. Most of the time they are estranged from everything, dodging the opinion of others that eventually arrives in the form of an evaluation, a peer review, or a blunt *No* or *Rejected*.
Claudia Rodríguez tells us in that episode how much she wanted to be a painter, but then, surprised by that isolation, she transitioned into becoming an art curator and professor.


## Art Curators and Scientific Journals

Curating art strikes me as a rather poetic profession, with many nuances. It is what would be, say, the conductor of an orchestra or, why not, the director of any production, whether theater or film. A curator chooses the exhibition space and the theme, selects the works, mounts them, and as a result of it all, a coherent reading of the artistic output of one or more artists emerges.
I think the curators of science could be scientific journals. At the end of the day, without those publications, it would seem that a scientist is still playing with paper and pen, making sketches without showing them to anyone. eLife is also a scientific research journal, focused on publishing work related to medicine and the life sciences (neuroscience, microbiology, physiology, etc.). However, a couple of years ago eLife decided to change the rules of the game of "Peer Review." It intervened differently in the selection of its _works_, where, within the valid or accepted framework, a peer review ends in either the acceptance or the rejection of a publication. eLife decided not only to reject publications, but to engage in dialogue with scientists and improve the work in synergy with them.
I see art curators doing this with artists. I don't know whether they do, as I lack that information, but knowing that the axis of their pursuit is to understand the artistic process and the concept, and to tell a story, it doesn't seem far-fetched to me that a curator-artist dialogue exists that improves the final result. Nor does it seem far-fetched that we could do science that way: less inhumane, more creative, more cooperative. Unfortunately, by declaring itself _inclusive_ in this respect, eLife lost its impact factor, a metric that determines year after year how much impact a journal had through its publications: how much it was cited, read, shared, demanded. In other words, its credibility. So perhaps **eLife is now looking for a curator**, under the performative yoke of yes/no and of the way we still evaluate scientific productivity and progress today.

The journal keeps running, but under a somewhat judgmental and distrustful gaze. Many people in science applauded this initiative, but it also unleashed the "I support you, but I don't consume you" effect, or in this case, "I won't publish with you." Some colleagues in training have told me that their papers end up in eLife as a "better than nothing," the consolation journal after several rejections elsewhere.
A system that works in art, _dialogue and accompaniment_, becomes unpopular and worthless in science. As if we truly enjoyed doing it the hard way.

>*Does anyone know since when we started doing it?*


## For the Love of Art

My first trip ever was to Valparaíso (_Valpo_), in central Chile, a very bohemian and artistic city. As a result, I met many artists of all nationalities and kinds of art. Each one left their mark and their story in my memory, but in relation to this topic, I remember Karla in particular, a very talented muralist who had traveled the world and was in Valparaíso at that moment, a city whose murals stand out in its urban art, to move her career forward. Finding a blank wall was not easy for her. She had worked hard on her sketches and her miniature plans, and when she finally found one, she could hardly contain her excitement. I saw her enjoying the search for the right tones, using a powdered paint she could dilute in white paint until she found the colors she wanted to bring to life. When she finished, she said nothing, and she didn't share the mural's address with us either, so we could go see it in person. It took her a few days to bring it up, and when she finally did, she told me she was deeply disappointed because she wasn't going to get paid anything, that this mural, like others in the city, was painted so that artists could make themselves known, without any financial compensation. **For the love of art**. She was furious, and she decided to go back to the finished mural and destroy it herself with *red paint*.

The love of art doesn't cover bills, or plane tickets back home, or food, or mental health, not even the materials used to paint it. But how beautiful all the murals painted for free in Valpo looked! How many new tourists arrived in Chile and headed to Valpo to visit them, how cheap it was to sell an artistic opportunity to the artists who were boosting their careers, or hoping to, there. The same happened with my musician friends, who lived off tips from people in the bars.

We scientists, I think, accept this dilemma under a sort of innocent ignorance, a rather **novice** one: working for a scientific laboratory, developing a project, investing your time and your parents' money, or perhaps your own savings, trusting that someday you will have a stable job, only to realize that you were swindled. _By whom? I still don't know_. Perhaps one simply falls in love with a profession that looks more like a pyramid scheme than anything else. Although there is some compensation, salaries are very low, not only as a student but also in the first steps as a young researcher, where you also find out that forever and ever you will have to ask for money so that someone funds your project. _Like being a musician singing in bars, waiting for someone to give you money so you can keep doing what you love_.

There is a kind of delirium in deciding to step out of that endless loop and look for a more stable life, or in staying, trusting that the next attempt, the next application, will accept you and you will be able to keep producing science, sending your work to another research journal, and then, of course, traveling to conferences, presenting your work, making yourself known and trying to convince others to come work with you or, if you did a good job of getting noticed, people will come to you on their own and want to work with you, but everyone will always end up in the same loop. It makes me dizzy to think about it, and I feel an urgency to warn people who haven't noticed yet, even my past self, back when I didn't know.

But how beautiful it looks to have a Google Scholar or a ResearchGate profile! Or to have awards and publications that probably contribute to less than 1% of the scientific relevance we take into account today.

>_This is where my pessimism ends in this entry._


## Call Backs and Rejections

Rejections are part of everyday life in any profession, it's true. But I have a feeling that artists (actors, for example) and scientists share the same source of frustration and, also, of resilience.

Imagine an actress who has studied theater and drama for years, and whose talent and knowledge fall to the background because they don't match the expectations of the character: thinner or more robust, taller or shorter, blonde, Black, Hispanic; with an English accent, or without one... She auditions a first time, then a second. She gets her _call back_, and that is where the director and the producer discover that, despite a great portfolio, she is missing one of the many requirements of the role. Days or weeks later comes the _rejection_. And the artist, after a long spiral of questioning, finally arrives at resilience: she accepts that this was not going to be her role, or perhaps that this film was never going to reach the screens.

I believe something similar happens in science, only here we hardly talk about talent, but rather about how intelligent and astute you are. Even so, the blow to the ego is the same, just as I imagine it is in the artistic world.

In recruitment, sometimes everything is very clear; other times, everything is so blurry that it is easy to never receive a final answer after a _call back_. As for feedback, forget it: only rarely will you get to know it. And even so, after a series of existential questions, resilience arrives and we accept that we like this so much, this continuous exposure and evaluation, that over time it gets less and less difficult to find your center, your base, to keep going on the path. It is, evidently, an extreme sport that only a flexible psyche can withstand. I have talked many times about the personality traits that the scientific world attracts, and yes, we all have a bit of weirdness, a bit of anxiety and shades of obsession that sometimes work as fuel for the engine and keep us moving.

In my case, every time I feel I've run out of gas, I go back to listening to [Karla Souza's talk](https://youtu.be/kz6GON4ykNo?si=CHk-vVONyZ1jETOC), where, among other stories, she highlights the importance of _remembering why you keep doing what you do_.

>_What is the fuel of your engine?_

## Abstraction of Reality

The creative process on both sides, the artist's and the scientist's, absorbs you so completely that it becomes your _Roman Empire_. And even though the world keeps turning, waves come in which reality falls away, letting you see that nobody cares whether you finish or not, whether that publication ever comes out, whether the grant they gave you yields results or not, or, on the artist's side, whether that painting is completed, whether you tear up the sketches that inspired you. The outcome will affect no one, and life will go on.

I don't yet know what the artist's consolation is under this reality, but the scientist's, I think, is that at least someone reads you, that what you produced is useful to someone else. Even if it won't be immediately recognized as fundamental enough for a major prize like a Nobel, perhaps it will inspire an idea or be discussed in some meeting. I think that is the consolation for staying absorbed and finishing what you started. Or there may be none, and everything may turn into an obsession that feeds your ego and individualism. I admit I have seen a lot of that in academia, too.

Bachelard, in *The Poetics of Space*, speaks through different analogies about the representation of walls in a space: walls that limit it, or that expand it when they are nonexistent. This idea leads into what emerges in his final chapters, where he turns to immensity, to the imagination as _"L'immensité intime"_:

>... comme l’immense n’est pas un objet, une phénoménologie de l’immense nous renverrait sans circuit à notre conscience imaginante.[...] Il apparaît alors clairement que les œuvres d’art sont les *sous-produits* de cet existentialisme de l’être imaginant. Dans cette voie de la rêverie d’immensité, le véritable *produit*, c’est la conscience d’agrandissement. Nous nous sentons promus à la dignité de l’être admirant. - *Chapter 8, p. 210*

Since immensity is not an object, it refers us back to our imagining consciousness. Here, works of art, scientific articles, written books and so on would be the products of the existentialism of a being who imagines. Entering the delirium of this immensity would yield the true _product_: the consciousness of expansion. _We feel lifted to the dignity of the being who admires what surrounds them._

Besides throwing a lifeline to my wretched existentialism, Bachelard offers one more thing, reminding me that we _open the world, in a certain way, in a going beyond the world itself as it is seen. Even while aware of ourselves, through the action of a brutal dialectic and creative expression, we become aware of grandeur_. We are then restored to a natural activity of our existence: _OBSERVING, ADMIRING, ABSTRACTING._
